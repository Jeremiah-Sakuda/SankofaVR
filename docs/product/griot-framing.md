# Griot framing for Sankofa

November 16, 2026. Separate framing proposal requested during the [five product review rounds](../reviews/2026-11-16-five-rubric-rounds.md). Runtime narration and release copy remain unchanged by this document.

**Sankofa invites you into the moment a memory passes from one person to another.** The griot gives that encounter its welcome and continuity. The visitor's meaningful action is choosing to listen to another perspective, then deciding what to keep. The water story makes this idea visible: someone who receives a memory eventually becomes the person carrying it.

Keep **For the stories you never got to hear** as the brand invitation. Follow it immediately with the concrete promise: **Sit beside a fire. Step into an illustrated telling. Hear the same meeting from another side.** This connects the emotional invitation to the selected river prototype's actual scope.

## Cultural foundation

West African griot traditions include demanding roles in storytelling, history, genealogy, poetry and music. The Metropolitan Museum's account describes how Sahelian epics are performed and adapted for successive audiences. This gives the metaphor substance: a teller connects an inherited account with people listening now. [The Met, on griots and the Sunjata epic](https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/sahel-sunjata-stories-songs).

Oral storytelling also belongs to a much wider range of African traditions. UNESCO describes oral expression as a means of transmitting knowledge, values and shared memory, sustained through relationships between people and generations. Its account of the Mijikenda of coastal Kenya offers a distinct example: oral traditions and performance are connected to community identity and the Kaya forests. These examples show why place, people and the circumstances of telling matter. [UNESCO on oral traditions](https://ich.unesco.org/en/oral-traditions-and-expressions-00053); [UNESCO on Mijikenda traditions](https://ich.unesco.org/en/USL/traditions-and-practices-associated-with-the-kayas-in-the-sacred-forests-of-the-mijikenda-00313).

Our creative interpretation is to make **listening a form of participation**. The app's fire is a fictional narrator inspired by griot traditions; it does not claim a hereditary office or community authority. Enkare Nairobi is the setting of this collection. Neither the West African griot tradition nor the coastal Mijikenda example is evidence for the fictional meeting, its dress, or its practices. The current [source register](../content/enkare-nairobi/sources.md) governs historical claims in the telling. Cultural consultation remains necessary before presenting this framing as community-endorsed.

## Audience and need

The original Sankofa began with the desire to feel connected to the lives behind a family or place name. Carry that motivation forward as an invitation to curiosity. A person can know a place's name while wanting to understand what everyday life there might have meant to someone. A short illustrated encounter gives that person a starting point for questions. This is the product hypothesis, not evidence of measured demand. [Original Sankofa inspiration](https://devpost.com/software/sankofa-y47f9p).

The prepared collection offers imagined people within sourced context. Its value is an encounter with perspective and uncertainty. It does not recover missing family testimony. Living storytellers and communities remain the people from whom real accounts can be learned.

## Pitch copy

Some stories reach us as fragments: a place name, an object, a question we never asked.

Sankofa begins with the desire to sit beside a telling and listen. Its fire is a storyteller inspired by West African griot traditions, where words and music carry memory across generations.

Choose a water pot and find yourself beside an imagined meeting near Enkare Nairobi in the 1850s. One woman has brought a younger traveller to a place she remembers. Hear one side, then choose the other. The meeting stays the same; your understanding changes.

Sourced history and imagined scenes remain clearly marked. When the telling ends, choose what to keep.

**Sankofa. For the stories you never got to hear.**

## Proposed cinematic opening

This is new proposed narration, approximately 35 seconds at an unhurried pace. It requires matching voice, captions and timing before implementation; it must not be substituted beneath the existing recordings.

> Come. Sit with me.
>
> A story needs someone to listen.
>
> In West African griot traditions, words and music carry memory between generations. That idea inspires the fire beside you.
>
> Tonight, our telling is set near Enkare Nairobi in the 1850s. The historical context is sourced. The people and their meeting are imagined.
>
> Choose the water pot. There is more than one side to hear.

Let the existing painted fire establish the invitation. Move into the image of hands and the pot as the invitation becomes personal. Give the place and truth boundary one clear card, then return control to the visitor. Preserve Skip and Pause. Do not explain the younger traveller's eventual role during the opening.

## Protect the emotional payoff

The older woman's uncertainty creates a vulnerable meeting. The younger traveller's choice of question changes what she receives from it. Years later she will carry the memory herself. That reversal is the strongest connection between the story and the griot framing; it should arrive through the authored telling.

In the demonstration, let the younger traveller's final sentence finish, then leave a short quiet interval before Keep. Avoid explanatory pitch narration over that line. A possible closing card, after the story has earned it, is: **First, she listens. One day, she is the one who remembers.**

The bead marks an explicitly kept perspective. Present it as a reminder of hearing someone, without suggesting that collecting all six preserves a culture or proves lasting engagement. An optional reflection for later product consideration is: **Who would you ask about a place they remember?** It requires no answer, recording or upload.

## How the framing survives scrutiny

| Challenge | Answer grounded in the current product |
|---|---|
| Why VR? | The pot opens the surrounding illustrated setting while the visitor stays seated. The two accounts belong to people occupying different places beside the visitor. Choosing whose side to hear connects attention, space and understanding. Whether this feels stronger than a film still needs newcomer and headset observation. |
| Is the griot merely a visual theme? | The narrator welcomes, locates the story, distinguishes evidence from imagination, and leaves room for the listener's curiosity. These duties should shape pacing and voice throughout the visit. |
| Does this equate Africa with one culture? | Name the West African inspiration and the specific Kenyan setting. Keep broader examples in supporting context; do not layer unrelated cultural symbols or music into the scene as generic authenticity. |
| Does uncertainty imply that oral history is unreliable? | The uncertainty belongs to one imagined person's recollection. It is not a verdict on oral traditions. Her willingness to acknowledge it is a character choice. |
| Is this an AI replacement for a tradition bearer? | Describe a fictional, griot-inspired guide. The present collection contains authored imagined encounters and marked historical context, with generated illustration and synthetic narration. Claims of preserving a living tradition require collaboration, permission and actual community work. |
| Is the promise larger than the experience? | Pair the emotional brand line with three prepared tellings, a named place and period, and the distinction between history and imagination. Keep ancestry recovery and invented founder memories out of the pitch. |

## What would validate the framing

Show a newcomer the opening without additional explanation and ask what they expect to experience, who the fire represents, and which parts they think are factual. After both perspectives, ask what changed in their understanding and what they would want to ask next. Success means they describe the encounter and another person's perspective. Expectations of recovered relatives or a factual historical recording indicate that the promise needs revision.
